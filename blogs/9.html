NOTES FROM A MADHOUSE

How do you add another dimension to one of the best games of 2009?

04-03-2010

What is it, to see the real world on a daily basis in three dimensions?
You can visually track objects based on where they are spatially located. So, you know if something is hidden behind another object, and you can almost tell how hard, soft, wet or cold a surface is based on how it looks.
Well, 3D technology has the same effect in games! You feel immersed in the environment and everything feels more true to life. The richer the game environment, the better the experience. The 3D technology loves thousands of polygons, high definition textures and complex geometry; it looks truer and every bit more alive!

Every major new video game is now produced in three dimensions. Everything in it is designed in three dimensions - the environment, the visual effects, the character and all the animations that go with them. You're looking at your game through a TV screen though, in two dimensions only: the result is flat.

TriOviz uses the third dimension information (which is already present in the game) to create the perspective and sense of immersion in a way that can be transmitted through any TV screen.
When you look at the resulting image, it's still flat. However, when you put on those glasses you'll instantly see the 3D effect!

Why is this? Because only the supplied glasses can decode the information we've stored in the image. What TriOviz does in the game works only with TriOviz glasses and vice-versa. Unlike other systems, TriOviz uses the third dimension information which already exists to perform visual tricks, such as modifying the 3D effect according to the part of the screen you are looking at. The camera focus works like your eyes. Move the camera to look at a specific object and it will be brought right into focus. If you slightly adjust the direction so you are now looking at the character down the hall, the entire 3D effect has been recalculated to accommodate your point of view.
The game engineers and programmers have specifically configured the effect so that it looks its best 100% of the time. TriOviz experts who also counsel Hollywood directors and CGI artists, have worked with the Rocksteady team to create the best experience possible, utilising the most of the TriOviz capabilities.
Batman: Arkham Asylum - Game Of The Year Edition will come bundled with two pairs of cardboard-coated 3D glasses so you can share the experience with a friend. If you do play with friends or family around don't worry, the 3-D mode does not scramble or spoil the image - so everyone will be able to watch you playing.
The TriOviz glasses are lightweight and slim. They fit snugly in the game box and you can even wear them on top of regular glasses if you wear them.
So in short, if you want to play the game in 3-D place the disc in the console tray and switch on your TV, that's it, it's that simple!

As with cinemas and movie theatres, it's always better with a bit of atmosphere. Dim the lights a little bit and play at a reasonable distance from the screen. We advise that 3 times the width of the screen is probably the best position for your eyes. You can sit anywhere up to that distance from the screen to get the best result.

Batman: Arkham Asylum - Game of the Year Edition offers a great 3D experience: detailed and up close characters look like they are standing in front you. Close combat has never been this exciting, watching the threats coming at you dynamically; you will really get caught up in the chaos of the Asylum. Of course, you can also play the game in standard mode. The 3D mode is there as an option that can be activated or deactivated at anytime directly from the in-game options menu. You can therefore choose to play the game in 3D for as long as you like.
When you activate the 3D mode, you will be notified by a small pictogram flashing on the screen. So, you can tell directly from the paused menu whether the game is in 3d mode or not quickly and conveniently.
There's no slowdown in the game and no shift in the colours. It's the same great game experience now in 3D. Enjoy.

When you play in 3D mode your brain will be sent more information. At the least, you'll get the feeling of a real third dimension. It will also make your eyes work more, so just like with every other game please take a break every hour or so.
There are also those who unfortunately will not experience anything. Colour blindness and different acuity for left and right eyes make it harder for some people to see in 3D. Medical studies show around one person out of ten might not experience 3D properly. We are sorry if that's you, but there's not much we can do about it regrettably.

So you may have heard or read about 3D gaming recently, it's certainly gaining popularity and attention in the video gaming world. Just like home cinema will never be like the 10m-wide screen and cutting edge surround sound systems found in movie theatres, 3D at home is not meant to top the theatre experience. Every movie theatre owner has invested thousands in order to make that upgrade to 3D. Even the polarised glasses that you wear in theatres come at around half the price of a newly released video game. We do everything that is possible to recreate the 3D experience in the best conditions and with the lowest accessibility barriers. A quality 3D experience, at home, for everyone - that's what we do.
Maybe you think the pair of glasses in your hand looks weird? Maybe you have that lukewarm memory of the gimmicky red and blue glasses found in cornflakes boxes? Well, that trick used to create 3D visuals from red and blue images was invented over a hundred years ago and is called anaglyph. It's very simple to achieve but the result is not at all convincing. The image looks grey and gives most people headaches quickly. We will not even go into moving images.
Be assured, we have done much better since then with improved techniques. What you're looking at is not red and blue anaglyph and we do not overlay coloured images.
It's called TriOviz, named after the process that does the job. It's time to look forward.

Alexis Arragon - Product Development Manager at Darkworks and the TriOviz team

During development, we get used to seeing the same levels, scenes and characters day in day out. Adding the 3D effect gives a fresh sense of excitement to those same characters and levels. Judging by the crowd at Rocksteady that had gathered (around a game that they are already very familiar with) to see the new 3D effect, it could cause quite a stir.

To make the 3D experience as complete and immersive as possible, we wanted to somehow add that effect to the pre-rendered cut scenes in Batman: Arkham Asylum. The original version of the game had already been through many iterations of optimisation to allow its cut scenes to play smoothly while level data was being loaded at the same time. This in turn, would make it challenging encoding the additional information to add depth to the scene.

We had to upgrade our movie render farm to output the additional depth information, and encode that depth into the movies. Some of our movies for the original release of Batman: Arkham Asylum had run through 3rd party post-processing software when they were originally created. This software didn't know how to handle the depth information that we had since created, so we had to find ways of passing that depth information through the post-process, by treating it as colour, then re-combine it with the 2D colour information to make 3D versions of the movies.
Adding this depth information to the movies required much iteration to find an optimal way of encoding the cut scenes with the additional depth information so they wouldn't exceed the available disk bandwidth, but didn't degrade visually too much when compared with the original version.

Retro-fitting the tech to the game was an interesting experience. Usually, as developers we choose which problems to engineer around and which to design around. Fitting a new technology to an old game means that a lot of decisions had already been made, so the only option is to tackle the problems directly, rather than being able to side step some of them.

Even though the TriOviz system has low overheads - shoe horning it into a finished game that has been through many optimisation cycles meant we had to get creative with finding more ways of offsetting the new memory costs (memory for buffers, GPU render time, etc.). Had we integrated the 3D technology at an early stage of development we might have chosen to have budgeted for these memory costs, rather than optimise other systems to offset the cost.

Hopefully the additional novelty of the 3D mode will entice some players to experience a great game that they might have otherwise overlooked!

John Gay - Engine Programmer at Rocksteady
